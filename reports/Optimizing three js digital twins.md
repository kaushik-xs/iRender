# Stop redrawing what the camera cannot change

A digital twin of 40-200 glTF clones gets to a steady 60 fps mostly by doing less redundant work. Hiding detail does much less. Three changes come first. Cache the directional shadow map, because orbiting the camera never invalidates it. Collapse per-clone draw calls with one InstancedMesh per asset part. Remove first-show hitches (shader compile and texture upload) before they reach the render loop. Hiding detail models while the camera moves, keeping only the structural geometry, is a weak trade at this scale. It saves draw calls that instancing removes anyway, and it causes the most visible kind of degradation: objects popping in and out mid-orbit. Autodesk had to ship a dedicated "Smooth Navigation" feature to fight exactly that ([APS blog](https://aps.autodesk.com/node/3443)). The production pattern worth copying is xeokit's: while the camera moves, cut the cost per pixel and switch off expensive effects, never whole objects, then restore everything about 0.5 s after the camera stops ([xeokit FastNavPlugin source](https://app.unpkg.com/@xeokit/xeokit-sdk@2.6.78/files/src/plugins/FastNavPlugin/FastNavPlugin.js)). Most of this plan costs no visual quality at all. Only the motion-time resolution drop is a deliberate, short-lived compromise. The research gives few measured gains, so each step has to be checked against the kit's own frame-time numbers.

## Stutter and low frame rate are separate problems with separate fixes

"Butter-smooth" fails in two ways, and they need different medicine. The first is sustained cost: every frame takes too long because of too many draw calls, a shadow pass re-rendered every frame, or high-DPR fill. The second is a hitch: a single frame that takes 100+ ms because a shader compiles, a texture uploads, or some JavaScript runs too long. The kit already sees the second kind. A cold first load produces one long task of 100-1400 ms. That fits synchronous program compile followed by driver queries that each wait for the GPU's command queue to drain. One production scene with 14 programs produced a **single 656 ms task**, and after warming its first frame dropped to "a few ms" ([three.js forum #94734](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/why-a-heavy-three-js-scene-tanks-pagespeed-and-it-is-not-your-javascript/94734)).

The budget is tight. At 60 Hz a frame has 16.7 ms, and on 120 Hz ProMotion displays it has 8.3 ms. That budget covers controls, path updates, tag projection, render submission and the browser's own compositing. Any task over **50 ms** counts as a long task ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/optimize-long-tasks)).

Measure before changing anything. `renderer.info` reports draw calls, triangles and program count, and needs `autoReset = false` when post-processing runs several passes ([three.js docs](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/WebGLRenderer.html)). stats-gl adds GPU timer queries, which need a feature flag on Safari ([stats-gl](https://github.com/RenaudRohlinger/stats-gl)). The Long Animation Frames API (Chrome 123+) catches hitches in the field and in CI ([Chrome LoAF docs](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/web-platform/long-animation-frames)). Profile with a fresh browser profile, because cached shader programs hide compile cost on repeat loads ([forum #94734](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/why-a-heavy-three-js-scene-tanks-pagespeed-and-it-is-not-your-javascript/94734)).

Program count is the best early warning of first-show hitches. Frame-time p95, p99 and max, gathered over a scripted orbit and a path animation, is the right acceptance metric. Average fps is not.

## Camera orbits never invalidate a directional shadow map

This is the largest lossless win available. A directional light's shadow map is rendered from the light's point of view. Orbiting the camera therefore changes nothing in it, provided the shadow camera is fixed over the plant and does not follow the view. Re-rendering it every frame is pure waste: a second depth pass over every caster. In one report, disabling `autoUpdate` **roughly halved the draw calls**. In another, a scene ran at 60 fps with static shadows but **fell to about 20 fps** once the map re-rendered on every change ([forum: shadowMap.autoUpdate](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/renderer-shadowmap-autoupdate-false/50401); [forum: shadowmap halves my fps](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/shadowmap-halves-my-fps/11519)).

The mechanism is `light.shadow.autoUpdate = false`, with `needsUpdate = true` set only when a caster or the light moves. Use the per-light flag rather than the old trick of setting `renderer.shadowMap.enabled = false` after the first render. That trick reportedly stopped caching the map after r164 ([forum](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/when-three-js-version-bigger-than-164-the-shadowmap-cant-be-cache-this-leads-to-a-decrease-in-rendering-performance/67687)). drei's `BakeShadows` helper ships the same idea as a default optimisation ([drei docs](https://drei.docs.pmnd.rs/performances/bake-shadows)). One gotcha: resizing the map while autoUpdate is off needs `shadow.map.setSize()` ([forum](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/shadowmap-not-resizing-correctly-when-scene-autoupdate-false/34474)). The kit's 2048 PCF choice is right on current three.js: since r182, `PCFSoftShadowMap` is deprecated on WebGLRenderer because plain `PCFShadowMap` is now soft ([Migration Guide](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/wiki/Migration-Guide)).

Objects moving along paths complicate this. If they cast into the big map, it has to update whenever they move, and forum consensus is that per-frame updates for moving casters defeat the purpose ([forum](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/many-lights-nearly-everything-static-shadows/9803)). There are three answers, in descending order of quality per millisecond:

- **Throttle the update.** Update the big map every 2nd-4th frame while things move, and update it immediately when movement stops. This cuts the shadow-pass cost to about a half or a quarter, in exchange for a shadow that lags its object by a frame or three. That lag is barely visible on slow conveyors and forklifts. No benchmark backs it; it is an inference.
- **Drop movers from the big map.** Static casters stay in a cached map that re-renders only on layout edits. Movers get a cheap fake: a blob decal, or a small contact shadow. ContactShadows is itself "a rather expensive effect" unless it is limited to a few frames ([drei ContactShadows](https://drei.docs.pmnd.rs/staging/contact-shadows)).
- **Freeze during camera motion.** If the camera and the movers are both moving, nobody's eye is tracking the shadow, so it can catch up when the camera rests.

Fitting the orthographic shadow camera tightly to the plant's bounds buys more sharpness than going to a 4096 map, which would cost four times the memory and fill. That is standard practice, though the notes have no source for it. A cached map is also independent of DPR. Once it stops re-rendering, the remaining shadow cost is the PCF lookups in the main pass, which the motion-time resolution drop described below also shrinks.

## Instancing turns roughly 1,000 draw calls into about 40

Deep-cloning a glTF per object multiplies draw calls: about 200 objects at about 5 primitives each is about 1,000 calls, and the shadow pass adds more on top. That reaches r3f's guidance of "no more than 1000 as the very maximum", where the target is "a few hundred or less" ([r3f docs](https://r3f.docs.pmnd.rs/advanced/scaling-performance)). Community mobile targets are 100-200 calls ([PlayCanvas guidelines](https://developer.playcanvas.com/user-manual/optimization/guidelines/)). In one case study, merging cut draw calls from **387 to 42** and mobile frame rate went from **10 to about 30 fps** ([forum](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/problems-with-performance-on-mobile-devices/23734)). A forum maintainer's ordering puts draw-call reduction first, triangles (LOD) second and per-frame JavaScript third. His estimate is that merging plus instancing "could yield a 10x-100x improvement" ([forum: when is LOD beneficial](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/when-is-it-actually-beneficial-to-use-lod-in-three-js-for-performance/87697)).

For a kit built from a handful of asset types, the right structure is one InstancedMesh per (asset type × glTF primitive). Eight types with five primitives each gives about 40 calls, whatever the object count. That makes the twin's cost flat as layouts grow from 40 to 200 objects. Moving an instance takes one `setMatrixAt` plus `instanceMatrix.needsUpdate`. For 200 instances the upload is about 12.8 KB per frame, which is trivial. Picking returns an `instanceId` ([three.js docs: InstancedMesh](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/InstancedMesh.html)).

Avoid BatchedMesh here. It is built for many distinct geometries that share one material. On WebGL it loops over every instance each frame to cull and sort. In issue #28776, InstancedMesh and merged geometry ran at about 120 fps where BatchedMesh managed about 30, and enabling `sortObjects` alone dropped one test from 30 to 9 fps ([three.js issue #28776](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/issues/28776)).

Instancing has three correctness traps:

- **Shared bounding sphere.** An InstancedMesh culls and raycasts against one bounding sphere that covers all its instances. Once movers leave their original positions, that sphere goes stale ([InstancedMesh.js](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mrdoob/three.js/dev/src/objects/InstancedMesh.js)). Recompute it, pad it to cover the paths, or set `frustumCulled = false` on meshes that contain movers.
- **Animated sub-parts.** A part with its own animated transform, such as a rotor, needs its own InstancedMesh whose instance matrices combine the object's transform with the part's.
- **Highlight and alarm glow.** Per-object material clones block instancing. Use a per-instance attribute instead. `instanceColor` multiplies the base colour, so it tints rather than glows. A real glow needs a per-instance emissive value patched in through `onBeforeCompile`, which keeps one program and one draw call. Alternatively, render the selected object separately, as donmccurdy suggests ([forum](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/when-is-it-actually-beneficial-to-use-lod-in-three-js-for-performance/87697)).

Keep OutlinePass restricted to the selection. A performance drop after r116 was traced to it including every object by default ([forum](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/performance-issues-with-three-outlinepass/7363)).

Hover picking is the other classic spike during motion. three-mesh-bvh makes raycasts logarithmic: "500 rays against an 80,000 polygon model at 60fps", and `firstHitOnly` speeds them up further ([three-mesh-bvh](https://github.com/gkjohnson/three-mesh-bvh)). With instancing, one BVH per shared geometry serves every instance. Throttle hover to one cast per frame, run it only against pickable roots, and skip it entirely while the camera is moving.

LOD comes later. It reduces triangles, not draw calls, and pays off only near "several million triangles per frame" ([forum](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/when-is-it-actually-beneficial-to-use-lod-in-three-js-for-performance/87697)). An inspection-style twin is usually viewed close up. If CAD exports are heavy, simplify the source asset once with meshoptimizer's error-bounded simplifier ([gltf-transform simplify](https://gltf-transform.dev/modules/functions/functions/simplify)). That is a bigger and safer win than runtime LOD switching.

## Hiding detail while moving trades a small saving for visible popping

The idea of hiding detail models while the camera moves and keeping only the structural geometry has real precedent. xeokit v3 culls objects whose solid angle falls below `solidAngleLimit` ([xeokit spatial.culling](https://xeokit.github.io/sdk/docs/api/modules/spatial.culling.html)). Autodesk's viewer draws geometry largest-on-screen first and spreads a frame over several ~17 ms slices while the user navigates ([InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/presentations/autodesk-forge-viewer)). Navisworks drops geometry to guarantee a frame rate ([Navisworks help](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/2020/ENU/Navisworks/files/GUID-1A87025C-F276-4978-847D-52CD63AF3F29.htm)).

Those systems handle 10^5-10^6 fragments, where draw-call count is the wall. With 40-200 objects behind about 40 instanced draw calls, hiding a third of the objects saves a few calls and some triangles. It does not touch the costs that actually scale with motion: fill rate at high DPR, PCF lookups and post-processing. Meanwhile the popping is the most noticeable artifact a viewer can produce. Autodesk's 2024 Smooth Navigation beta exists specifically because of "mesh popping and flickering" ([APS blog](https://aps.autodesk.com/node/3443)). No source quantified how noticeable motion-only culling is, or what it gains in three.js.

The better-documented pattern degrades effects, not objects. xeokit's FastNavPlugin, on every camera matrix change, switches off edges, SAO, PBR and colour textures. It can optionally hide transparent objects and scale canvas resolution, by default to 0.6. It restores everything **0.5 s** after the last change, and that delay "prevents rapid flipping between low and high quality" ([xeokit blog](https://xeokit.io/blog/smoother-navigation-with-fastnavplugin); [source](https://app.unpkg.com/@xeokit/xeokit-sdk@2.6.78/files/src/plugins/FastNavPlugin/FastNavPlugin.js)). It explicitly "does not hide X-rayed, selected, highlighted objects" ([API docs](https://xeokit.github.io/xeokit-sdk/docs/class/src/plugins/FastNavPlugin/FastNavPlugin.js~FastNavPlugin.html)). r3f's `regress()` follows the same pattern: drop quality on controls `change`, restore after a **200 ms** debounce, and in its example lower DPR and skip AO until the camera is still ([r3f docs](https://r3f.docs.pmnd.rs/advanced/scaling-performance)). Cesium's 0.2 s `foveatedTimeDelay` ([Cesium3DTileset](https://cesium.com/learn/cesiumjs/ref-doc/Cesium3DTileset.html)) and xeokit's 0.5 s bracket a sensible restore window.

Resolution is the motion-time lever with the most effect. Fragment cost grows with the square of DPR: DPR 2 means four times the pixels of DPR 1 ([react.doctor](https://www.react.doctor/docs/rules/react-doctor/three-cap-device-pixel-ratio)). A 0.5 factor means 75% fewer pixels and looks "slightly blurry" ([xeokit blog](https://xeokit.io/blog/smoother-navigation-with-fastnavplugin)). For this kit, cap the still DPR at 2 on desktop and 1.5 on mobile. While the camera moves, render at about 0.6-0.75 of that.

The quality cost is real but brief. Motion blur in the eye masks it, and the sharp frame returns within half a second. The engineering cost: `setPixelRatio` reallocates the drawing buffer and any composer targets. Switch once on motion start and once on restore, never every frame. An fps-driven governor in the style of PerformanceMonitor can choose the still DPR per device, using hysteresis and a flip-flop limit to stop oscillation ([drei PerformanceMonitor](http://drei.docs.pmnd.rs/performances/performance-monitor)).

If the kit adds detail hiding anyway, perhaps for very heavy CAD plants, the evidence supports a narrow version:

- **Opt-in only.** Hide only objects explicitly tagged as detail (bolts, cable trays, small sensors), and only when their projected bounding sphere is below a pixel threshold.
- **Protected objects stay.** Never hide selected, alarmed or tagged objects.
- **Restore during the rest delay.** That way objects reappear while the eye is not tracking motion.
- **Fade them back in.** A screen-door dither over 200-500 ms works. Cesium uses dithered transitions of 0.5 s that have "essentially no overdraw" and need no depth sort ([Cesium blog](https://cesium.com/blog/2022/10/20/smoother-lod-transitions-in-cesium-for-unreal/)).

Even then it should come last in the plan, behind fixes that cost nothing visible.

DOM tags need the same discipline. Projected tags are fine at tens, not hundreds. drei `<Html>` fell below 5 fps past about 50 elements in Chrome ([forum](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/many-html-elements-from-three-js-fiber-drei-causes-low-framerate-in-chrome/28540)). CSS2DRenderer took 80% of one profile because it walks the scene and writes styles on every render ([forum](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/does-css2drenderer-ignore-automatrixupdate-false/67064)). The kit's approach is the cheap end already: manual projection, transform-only writes and cached sizes. The remaining wins:

- **Skip idle frames.** Do no tag work on frames where neither the camera matrix nor any tagged object moved.
- **Hide off-screen tags.** Hide tags behind the camera or off-screen inside the same write pass.
- **Reuse scratch vectors.** Avoid allocating per tag per frame.
- **Do not hide tags during motion.** Tags are what users track while orbiting. Throttling them makes them "swim" against the 3D view, which looks worse than any fill-rate saving is worth.

troika SDF text has handled 100+ labels at over 140 fps ([forum](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/100-dynamic-troika-three-text-labels-with-1s-updates/90287)). It is the escape hatch if tag counts ever reach the hundreds.

## First-show hitches come from compile and upload, not from frame cost

Smooth camera motion is wasted if the first orbit toward a new machine freezes for 300 ms. Program count depends on the cache key, not on how many materials exist. Clones with identical settings share one program. Toggling `transparent`, `side`, maps, defines, light counts or the render target's colour space creates a new one ([WebGLPrograms.js](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mrdoob/three.js/dev/src/renderers/webgl/WebGLPrograms.js)). The kit's highlight, ghost and x-ray variants are therefore latent hitches. Each one compiles the first time a user selects something. The fix, step by step:

1. Fix the lighting, environment, shadow and tone-mapping setup before loading anything, because all of them are part of the program key.
2. For each new model type, `await renderer.compileAsync(model, camera, scene)` before adding it ([three.js docs](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/WebGLRenderer.html)).
3. Pre-compile every highlight variant once at startup.
4. Upload textures ahead of time with `renderer.initTexture()`.

`compileAsync` is only non-blocking where KHR_parallel_shader_compile works. Chrome supports it, Firefox does not ([caniuse](https://caniuse.com/wf-khr-parallel-shader-compile)), and Safari on Apple Silicon has a reported blocking bug ([WebKit 251514](https://lists.webkit.org/pipermail/webkit-unassigned/2023-February/1084921.html)). It also does not upload textures. One team removed first-seconds stutter only by doing a one-off render with every mesh forced visible and unculled ([forum #86191](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/reduce-first-render-lag-renderer-compileasync-is-not-doing-anything/86191)).

Textures are the second hitch. WebP is decoded to full RGBA and uploaded uncompressed: a 4096² texture occupies **about 90 MB** whatever its file size. KTX2/Basis cuts memory 4-8x and makes uploads 4-8x faster ([Don McCurdy](https://www.donmccurdy.com/2024/02/11/web-texture-formats/)). Converting reused textures of 1K and above to KTX2 is a quality-neutral fix: ETC1S for colour, UASTC for normal maps. Spread uploads and object additions across frames. Use `scheduler.yield()` where it exists; Safari lacks it and needs a fallback ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/optimize-long-tasks)).

Two popular escape routes do not fit this kit yet:

- **WebGPURenderer is currently slower on the CPU for many-mesh scenes.** An r183 benchmark measured **9.7 ms vs 4.5 ms** per frame and a **167 ms vs 32 ms** cold first frame against WebGLRenderer ([forum](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/webgpurenderer-2x-slower-cpu-and-5-10x-slower-first-frame-than-webglrenderer-on-many-mesh-scenes-r183-same-on-both-backends/91904)).
- **OffscreenCanvas in a worker isolates jank but does not remove it.** Tags positioned on the main thread from posted coordinates would lag the 3D view by a frame during motion ([web.dev](https://web.dev/articles/offscreen-canvas)). It is worth trying only if main-thread long tasks are proven to be the cause.

## The prioritized plan

Order matters. Lossless fixes go first, and quality compromises only once they are measured and still needed.

| # | Technique | Cost to build | What it saves | Quality compromise |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Instrumentation: `renderer.info`, stats-gl, LoAF, a scripted orbit and path benchmark tracking p95/p99/max frame time | Low | Nothing directly. It tells you which of steps 1-8 matter | None |
| 1 | Cap DPR (2 desktop, 1.5 mobile); one coalesced `needsFrame` loop that keeps rendering until damping settles | Low | Up to 2.25-4x fill on high-DPR screens; idle frames drop to zero | Negligible at the caps |
| 2 | Cache the shadow map (`light.shadow.autoUpdate=false`), fixed and tightly fitted shadow camera, `needsUpdate` only when casters move | Low | A whole caster depth pass on every camera-only frame, roughly half the draw calls in one report | None during orbit |
| 3 | Movers: throttle shadow updates (every 2-4 frames, catch up at rest) or blob shadows | Low-medium | 50-75% of the shadow cost while things move | A shadow lagging 1-3 frames, or simpler shadows on movers |
| 4 | Pre-warm: `compileAsync` per model type plus highlight variants, `initTexture`, KTX2 for textures ≥1K, a frame-budgeted add queue | Medium | The 100-1400 ms first-show and first-select hitches | None (UASTC on normal maps) |
| 5 | One InstancedMesh per asset part, per-instance highlight and emissive attribute, shared materials, padded bounds for movers, BVH picking throttled to one per frame and off during motion | High | About 1,000 calls down to about 40, flat as layouts grow; pointermove spikes | None if the glow is ported correctly |
| 6 | Fast-nav mode on controls `change`: DPR × 0.6-0.75, skip SSAO, outline and edge passes, restore 300-500 ms after the last change | Low-medium | Most fill and post-processing cost during motion | Slight blur and missing effects, while moving only |
| 7 | Tags: skip idle frames, hide off-screen tags, reuse scratch vectors | Low | CPU per frame, layout risk | None |
| 8 | Optional: hide tagged small detail below N px during motion, with a dither fade back in | Medium | A few draw calls and triangles | Popping risk; never apply it to selected or alarmed objects |

Steps 1, 2 and 7 are near-free and lossless, so ship them first. Step 4 fixes the stutter users notice most. Step 5 is the structural investment that keeps the kit smooth at 200 objects on laptops and phones. Step 6 is the only deliberate quality trade worth making by default. Step 8 should stay off unless measurements on a heavy CAD plant show the frame still misses its budget after steps 1-7.

The evidence has gaps. No source published per-frame millisecond savings for fast-nav modes, AdaptiveDpr or motion-time culling. No benchmark compares instancing with clones at 40-200 objects. Most numbers above come from forum reports, not controlled tests. That is why step 0 comes first: every row above should earn its place in the kit's own frame-time traces.

## Conclusion

Most of the kit's frame budget goes to work that camera motion never required: re-shadowing a static plant, issuing a draw call per clone primitive, and compiling or uploading on first sight. Hiding the scene's detail treats a symptom that a 40-200 object twin mostly does not have, and it pays for it in the artifact users notice most. The useful rule from xeokit and Autodesk is to degrade how each pixel is shaded, never what is in the picture, and to protect anything the user is tracking (selection, alarms, tags) absolutely.

A second point follows from the shadow analysis. Moving parts and a moving camera are different cost problems. Camera motion is almost free for shadows and expensive in fill. Moving objects are cheap to transform but expensive in shadow updates. Budgeting the two separately, rather than under one "something is moving, render everything" flag, lets the kit stay at 60 fps without giving up visual quality when the view is at rest.
